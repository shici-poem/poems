# 中文名 | English name | dynasty | dates | short bio  (chronological order)
诗经 | The Book of Songs | 先秦 | c. 1000–600 BC | China's oldest anthology of poetry: 305 songs from the Western Zhou to the Spring and Autumn period, gathered — tradition says — by Confucius. Folk songs, court odes and temple hymns, mostly in four-character lines, with the refrains and nature images that shaped all later Chinese verse.
屈原 | Qu Yuan | 先秦 | c. 340–278 BC | Minister of the state of Chu, slandered and exiled, who drowned himself in the Miluo River when Chu fell to Qin. The first great named poet of China; his passionate, shamanic *Songs of Chu* gave Chinese poetry its voice of the loyal man wronged. The Dragon Boat Festival honours him.
项羽 | Xiang Yu | 秦 | 232–202 BC | The "Hegemon-King of Western Chu", the fearsome general who destroyed the Qin dynasty and then lost the empire to Liu Bang. Surrounded at Gaixia, he sang one song to his consort Yu before his last stand.
刘邦 | Liu Bang | 汉 | 256–195 BC | A village constable who rose to found the Han dynasty (206 BC – AD 220) as Emperor Gaozu. His only surviving poem was sung at a homecoming feast after he had won the empire.
李延年 | Li Yannian | 汉 | d. c. 87 BC | Court musician to Emperor Wu of Han and director of the Music Bureau (Yuefu). His song about a matchless beauty brought his sister to the emperor's side.
汉乐府 | Han Yuefu Ballads | 汉 | 206 BC–AD 220 | Anonymous songs collected by the Han Music Bureau (*yuefu*) — the voices of farmers, soldiers, lovers and wives. Plain, vivid and direct, they founded the ballad tradition that poets imitated for two thousand years.
古诗十九首 | Nineteen Old Poems | 汉 | c. 25–220 | Nineteen anonymous five-character poems of the late Eastern Han, on parting, longing and the brevity of life. Later critics called them "the crown of five-character verse".
曹操 | Cao Cao | 魏晋 | 155–220 | Warlord, statesman and strategist of the Three Kingdoms era who unified north China and founded the state of Wei. Also a powerful poet whose stark, heroic verses set the tone of the Jian'an age.
曹植 | Cao Zhi | 魏晋 | 192–232 | Brilliant son of Cao Cao, the most celebrated poet of his age — "of all the world's talent, he had eight parts in ten". Distrusted and persecuted by his brother, Emperor Wen of Wei.
陶渊明 | Tao Yuanming | 魏晋 | 365–427 | Also called Tao Qian. Gave up official life rather than "bow for five pecks of rice", and farmed in poverty with his chrysanthemums and wine. The father of Chinese pastoral poetry, revered for his simplicity and integrity.
北朝民歌 | Northern Dynasties Folk Song | 南北朝 | 4th–6th century | Anonymous songs of the northern steppe kingdoms — vast skies, horses and heroic women — preserved in the Song-dynasty anthology *Yuefu Shiji*.
陆凯 | Lu Kai | 南北朝 | d. 504 | Northern Wei official remembered for one tiny poem, sent with a sprig of plum blossom to a friend far away — it made "a branch of spring" a synonym for a gift between friends.
虞世南 | Yu Shinan | 唐 | 558–638 | Statesman and one of the four great calligraphers of the early Tang, trusted counsellor of Emperor Taizong.
王绩 | Wang Ji | 唐 | c. 590–644 | Early Tang recluse and wine lover who modelled himself on Tao Yuanming; his plain regulated verse pointed away from courtly ornament.
骆宾王 | Luo Binwang | 唐 | c. 638–684 | One of the "Four Paragons of the Early Tang". Wrote a famous poem about a goose at the age of seven; later composed the call to arms against Empress Wu, after which he vanished.
王勃 | Wang Bo | 唐 | c. 650–676 | The most dazzling of the "Four Paragons of the Early Tang", a prodigy who wrote the *Preface to the Pavilion of Prince Teng*. He drowned at about 26 crossing the sea to visit his father.
杨炯 | Yang Jiong | 唐 | 650–c. 693 | One of the "Four Paragons of the Early Tang", a child prodigy known for his vigorous frontier poems.
李峤 | Li Qiao | 唐 | c. 645–714 | Early Tang chief minister and prolific poet of riddling object-poems, famous for his little poem on the wind.
宋之问 | Song Zhiwen | 唐 | c. 656–712 | Court poet who, with Shen Quanqi, perfected the rules of regulated verse. Exiled to the far south, he wrote a famous quatrain on coming home.
陈子昂 | Chen Zi'ang | 唐 | 661–702 | Early Tang reformer who called poets back from court prettiness to the strength of the Han and Wei. His four lines on Youzhou Terrace are one of the loneliest cries in Chinese verse.
贺知章 | He Zhizhang | 唐 | 659–744 | Witty, wine-loving court official who called Li Bai "an immortal banished from heaven". Retired home at 85 after fifty years away and wrote about it.
张若虚 | Zhang Ruoxu | 唐 | c. 660–c. 720 | Known for only two surviving poems — one of them, *Spring River in the Flower Moon Night*, is called "the poem that overtops the whole Tang".
张九龄 | Zhang Jiuling | 唐 | 678–740 | Upright chief minister under Emperor Xuanzong, a southerner of great integrity whose dismissal marked the start of the court's decline. Patron of Wang Wei and Meng Haoran.
王之涣 | Wang Zhihuan | 唐 | 688–742 | High Tang poet of whom only six poems survive — but two of them, on Stork Tower and the Liangzhou frontier, are among the most famous ever written.
孟浩然 | Meng Haoran | 唐 | 689–740 | The great landscape poet of the High Tang who never held office, living as a recluse near Xiangyang. Friend of Li Bai and Wang Wei, whose name is always paired with his ("Wang–Meng").
王翰 | Wang Han | 唐 | 687–726 | Wealthy, flamboyant High Tang poet; one wine-soaked frontier quatrain made him immortal.
王湾 | Wang Wan | 唐 | 693–751 | Early High Tang official; one couplet of his, on the sun born from the sea before night has ended, was hung by the chief minister in his hall as a model of poetry.
王昌龄 | Wang Changling | 唐 | c. 698–757 | Called "the Lord of the Seven-character Quatrain". Master of frontier poems and of the laments of palace women; twice exiled, killed during the An Lushan rebellion.
李颀 | Li Qi | 唐 | c. 690–751 | High Tang poet of frontier ballads and of music, famed for his portraits of musicians and soldiers.
祖咏 | Zu Yong | 唐 | 699–c. 746 | Friend of Wang Wei. Asked to write a twelve-line exam poem, he stopped after four, saying "the meaning is complete" — and those four lines survive.
崔颢 | Cui Hao | 唐 | 704–754 | His *Yellow Crane Tower* so impressed Li Bai that Li Bai declined to write about the place: "Cui Hao's poem is already above it."
王维 | Wang Wei | 唐 | 701–761 | Poet, painter, musician and devout Buddhist — "in his poems there are paintings, in his paintings poems". Master of quiet landscape verse written at his Wangchuan estate. Called the "Poet Buddha".
李白 | Li Bai | 唐 | 701–762 | Also Li Po. The "Immortal of Poetry": a free spirit who wandered the empire with sword and wine jug, served briefly at Emperor Xuanzong's court, and wrote with dazzling spontaneity of the moon, mountains, friendship and wine. With Du Fu, the greatest of Chinese poets.
高适 | Gao Shi | 唐 | c. 704–765 | Frontier poet who, unusually for a poet, rose to high military command. His verse is bold and generous-hearted.
常建 | Chang Jian | 唐 | fl. 727 | Minor official who withdrew to live as a recluse; remembered for one perfect temple poem.
刘长卿 | Liu Changqing | 唐 | c. 726–c. 786 | Master of the five-character line ("the Great Wall of five-character verse"), twice exiled; his poems are spare, wintry and clear.
杜甫 | Du Fu | 唐 | 712–770 | The "Sage of Poetry". Lived through the An Lushan rebellion that shattered the Tang, and recorded it — war, famine, refugees, his own poverty — with unmatched compassion and craft. His poems are called "poetic history".
岑参 | Cen Shen | 唐 | c. 715–770 | Spent years in the far western garrisons of Central Asia; the greatest painter in words of deserts, snowstorms and the frontier.
张继 | Zhang Ji | 唐 | fl. 753 | Mid-Tang official known almost entirely for one quatrain, written at Maple Bridge near Suzhou — a poem that made a temple bell famous for 1,200 years.
张志和 | Zhang Zhihe | 唐 | 732–774 | Court scholar turned recluse who called himself "the Old Fisherman of the Misty Waves"; his fishing song is one of the earliest *ci* lyrics.
韩翃 | Han Hong | 唐 | fl. 754 | One of the "Ten Talents of the Dali era"; his Cold Food Festival poem was a favourite of the emperor.
韦应物 | Wei Yingwu | 唐 | c. 737–792 | Once a swaggering palace guard, he reformed and became a gentle, reclusive official-poet in the line of Tao Yuanming and Wang Wei.
卢纶 | Lu Lun | 唐 | c. 739–799 | One of the "Ten Talents of the Dali era", known for his crisp frontier songs.
司空曙 | Sikong Shu | 唐 | c. 720–790 | One of the "Ten Talents of the Dali era"; master of understated poems of friendship and poverty.
李端 | Li Duan | 唐 | c. 743–782 | One of the "Ten Talents of the Dali era", remembered for a sly little poem about a zither player.
李益 | Li Yi | 唐 | 746–829 | Mid-Tang frontier poet whose quatrains were set to music and sung across the empire.
孟郊 | Meng Jiao | 唐 | 751–814 | Poor, bitter, austere poet and friend of Han Yu, who passed the examination only at 46. His poem for his mother is the most loved poem on a mother's love.
张籍 | Zhang Ji | 唐 | c. 766–830 | Mid-Tang poet and friend of Han Yu and Bai Juyi, champion of plain "new yuefu" ballads about ordinary lives.
王建 | Wang Jian | 唐 | c. 767–830 | Mid-Tang poet of village life and the palace, writing in a plain ballad style alongside Zhang Ji.
韩愈 | Han Yu | 唐 | 768–824 | The great prose master and Confucian reformer of the mid Tang; as a poet, bold and unconventional. Exiled for protesting against the worship of a Buddha relic.
刘禹锡 | Liu Yuxi | 唐 | 772–842 | Called the "Poet Hero". A reformer exiled for over twenty years who never lost his wit or defiance; he adapted southern folk songs into his "Bamboo Branch" lyrics.
白居易 | Bai Juyi | 唐 | 772–846 | Also Po Chü-i. Believed poems should be understood by everyone — he read drafts to an old woman and rewrote what she could not follow. Author of *The Song of Everlasting Sorrow* and *The Pipa Player*, and of satires on the people's suffering.
李绅 | Li Shen | 唐 | 772–846 | Mid-Tang chief minister, a friend of Bai Juyi and Yuan Zhen; his two short poems on farmers are learned by every Chinese schoolchild.
崔护 | Cui Hu | 唐 | 772–846 | Mid-Tang official remembered for a single quatrain about a girl's face among peach blossoms.
柳宗元 | Liu Zongyuan | 唐 | 773–819 | Brilliant reformer exiled to the wild south for the rest of his life; great prose writer and a poet of solitary, crystalline landscapes.
元稹 | Yuan Zhen | 唐 | 779–831 | Bai Juyi's closest friend and co-founder of the "new yuefu" movement; his elegies for his wife are among the most moving in Chinese.
贾岛 | Jia Dao | 唐 | 779–843 | A former monk famous for agonising over single words — the story of him choosing between "push" and "knock" gave Chinese the word for careful revision, *tuiqiao*.
胡令能 | Hu Lingneng | 唐 | 785–826 | A tinker and recluse of Henan whose few surviving poems are full of fresh country scenes.
张祜 | Zhang Hu | 唐 | c. 785–c. 849 | Free-spirited mid-Tang poet who never held office; his palace poem was sung until listeners wept.
朱庆馀 | Zhu Qingyu | 唐 | fl. 826 | Mid-Tang poet who sent a famous coded poem to his examiner Zhang Ji before the exams.
李贺 | Li He | 唐 | 790–816 | The "Ghost of Poetry": a sickly genius who rode out each day on a donkey, dropping lines into a brocade bag. Died at 26, leaving strange, dark, dazzling poems.
杜秋娘 | Du Qiuniang | 唐 | fl. early 9th century | A singing girl of Jinling who became a consort in the Tang palace; her song "The Gold-Threaded Robe" is a famous call to seize the day.
金昌绪 | Jin Changxu | 唐 | fl. 9th century | A poet of whom we know nothing except one perfect little poem.
许浑 | Xu Hun | 唐 | c. 791–858 | Late Tang poet famous for his images of water and rain; "the mountain rain is coming and the wind fills the tower" is his.
杜牧 | Du Mu | 唐 | 803–852 | The "Little Du" (to Du Fu's "Old Du"). A brilliant, romantic late Tang poet of Yangzhou nights and southern landscapes, and a sharp critic of history.
温庭筠 | Wen Tingyun | 唐 | c. 812–c. 866 | Late Tang poet and pioneer of the *ci* lyric, leader of the "Huajian" school of richly ornamented love songs.
李商隐 | Li Shangyin | 唐 | c. 813–858 | The most mysterious of Chinese poets — his "Untitled" love poems are dense with allusion and longing, and still argued over a thousand years later.
陈陶 | Chen Tao | 唐 | c. 812–c. 885 | Late Tang poet and recluse whose one great frontier quatrain is among the saddest war poems in Chinese.
赵嘏 | Zhao Gu | 唐 | c. 806–c. 853 | Late Tang poet much admired by Du Mu for his lines on autumn and rain.
曹松 | Cao Song | 唐 | c. 828–903 | Late Tang poet who passed the exams in his seventies; one line of his became a proverb against war.
罗隐 | Luo Yin | 唐 | 833–910 | Failed the examinations ten times; a bitter, witty satirist of the late Tang.
聂夷中 | Nie Yizhong | 唐 | 837–c. 884 | Late Tang poet of humble birth who wrote with sympathy of the peasants' plight.
黄巢 | Huang Chao | 唐 | 820–884 | A salt smuggler who failed the examinations and led the great rebellion that captured Chang'an and doomed the Tang.
韦庄 | Wei Zhuang | 唐 | c. 836–910 | Late Tang poet who lived through the fall of the dynasty and became chief minister of Former Shu; a master of the early *ci*.
秦韬玉 | Qin Taoyu | 唐 | fl. 880s | Late Tang poet whose poem "The Poor Girl" gave Chinese the phrase "making wedding dresses for others".
高骈 | Gao Pian | 唐 | 821–887 | Late Tang general and governor, also a refined poet.
王驾 | Wang Jia | 唐 | b. 851 | Late Tang poet known for a few delicate quatrains on spring.
李璟 | Li Jing | 五代 | 916–961 | Second ruler of the Southern Tang kingdom and father of Li Yu; a gifted *ci* poet.
冯延巳 | Feng Yansi | 五代 | 903–960 | Chief minister of the Southern Tang and one of the great early *ci* poets, bridging the Huajian school and the Song masters.
李煜 | Li Yu | 五代 | 937–978 | Last ruler of the Southern Tang, captured by the Song in 975 and poisoned three years later. His lyrics of lost kingdom and endless sorrow made him the "Emperor of *Ci*".
林逋 | Lin Bu | 宋 | 967–1028 | Recluse who lived for twenty years on Solitary Hill by West Lake, never marrying — "plum blossoms were his wife and cranes his children".
范仲淹 | Fan Zhongyan | 宋 | 989–1052 | Statesman, reformer and frontier commander of the Northern Song, whose motto was "to be first to worry about the world's worries, and last to enjoy its joys".
柳永 | Liu Yong | 宋 | c. 984–c. 1053 | Northern Song *ci* poet of the pleasure quarters, who developed the long slow lyric; "wherever there is a well, people sing Liu Yong".
张先 | Zhang Xian | 宋 | 990–1078 | Northern Song *ci* poet nicknamed "Zhang of the Three Shadows" for his lines on shadows.
晏殊 | Yan Shu | 宋 | 991–1055 | Child prodigy who became chief minister of the Northern Song; his polished, gently melancholy *ci* lyrics are models of the form.
宋祁 | Song Qi | 宋 | 998–1061 | Northern Song historian and poet, called "the Red Apricot Minister" after one famous line.
欧阳修 | Ouyang Xiu | 宋 | 1007–1072 | The towering literary statesman of the Northern Song — historian, essayist, poet and mentor of Su Shi; "the Old Drunkard".
曾巩 | Zeng Gong | 宋 | 1019–1083 | One of the "Eight Great Prose Masters of the Tang and Song", a student of Ouyang Xiu.
王安石 | Wang Anshi | 宋 | 1021–1086 | Chief minister and radical reformer of the Northern Song, and a poet of exquisite, carefully chiselled quatrains.
王令 | Wang Ling | 宋 | 1032–1059 | Precocious Northern Song poet admired by Wang Anshi; died at 27.
程颢 | Cheng Hao | 宋 | 1032–1085 | Neo-Confucian philosopher, one of the "Two Chengs" who shaped the thought of later China.
王观 | Wang Guan | 宋 | 1035–1100 | Northern Song *ci* poet known for a witty farewell song.
苏轼 | Su Shi | 宋 | 1037–1101 | Also Su Dongpo. The universal genius of the Song — poet, essayist, painter, calligrapher, statesman, gourmet — repeatedly exiled for his outspokenness and serene through it all. His bold lyrics opened up the *ci* to every subject.
晏几道 | Yan Jidao | 宋 | 1038–1110 | Son of Yan Shu; a proud, unworldly poet of love and memory whose lyrics rival Li Yu's for melancholy.
黄庭坚 | Huang Tingjian | 宋 | 1045–1105 | Su Shi's disciple and friend, founder of the Jiangxi school of poetry and a great calligrapher.
李之仪 | Li Zhiyi | 宋 | 1048–1117 | Northern Song official and friend of Su Shi, remembered for one folk-song-like lyric of love by the Yangtze.
秦观 | Qin Guan | 宋 | 1049–1100 | One of the "Four Scholars of Su's School", master of tender, melancholy love lyrics.
贺铸 | He Zhu | 宋 | 1052–1125 | Northern Song *ci* poet, an ugly, gallant man known as "He of the Plum Rain" for one line.
周邦彦 | Zhou Bangyan | 宋 | 1056–1121 | Director of the imperial music bureau, the great craftsman of the Song *ci*, renowned for exquisite music and diction.
李清照 | Li Qingzhao | 宋 | 1084–c. 1155 | The greatest woman poet in Chinese. Her early lyrics are bright and playful; after the Jurchen invasion and her husband's death, she wrote some of the most heartbroken poems in the language.
曾几 | Zeng Ji | 宋 | 1085–1166 | Southern Song poet and teacher of Lu You.
李重元 | Li Chongyuan | 宋 | fl. 1122 | Northern Song poet whose four "Remembering the Prince" lyrics survive.
陈与义 | Chen Yuyi | 宋 | 1090–1138 | Poet who fled south when the Jurchen took north China; his later poems are full of the loss.
岳飞 | Yue Fei | 宋 | 1103–1142 | The Southern Song general who fought the Jurchen Jin to recover the north, and was put to death on false charges by Qin Hui. The supreme symbol of loyalty in Chinese culture.
陆游 | Lu You | 宋 | 1125–1210 | The most prolific of all Chinese poets (nearly 10,000 surviving poems), a patriot who longed all his long life to see the north recovered. His one great love, Tang Wan, was taken from him by his mother.
唐琬 | Tang Wan | 宋 | c. 1128–1156 | Lu You's cousin and first wife, forced to divorce him by his mother. Meeting him by chance in Shen Garden years later, she answered his poem with her own — and died soon after.
范成大 | Fan Chengda | 宋 | 1126–1193 | Southern Song statesman whose sixty "Seasonal Pastoral" poems are the fullest picture of Chinese farm life in verse.
杨万里 | Yang Wanli | 宋 | 1127–1206 | Southern Song poet of nature's small wonders — a dragonfly, a pond, a child chasing a butterfly — in a fresh, lively, humorous style.
朱熹 | Zhu Xi | 宋 | 1130–1200 | The greatest Neo-Confucian philosopher, whose commentaries on the classics were the basis of the civil exams for seven centuries; his short poems turn ideas into images.
张孝祥 | Zhang Xiaoxiang | 宋 | 1132–1170 | Top graduate of his year who championed the war against the Jin; a bold lyricist in the manner of Su Shi.
辛弃疾 | Xin Qiji | 宋 | 1140–1207 | Born under Jurchen rule, he led a daring raid and fled south at 22 to serve the Song, but was given no army and spent decades in retirement. The greatest heroic *ci* poet, paired with Su Shi.
姜夔 | Jiang Kui | 宋 | c. 1155–1221 | Southern Song musician and poet who composed his own tunes; his lyrics are cool, crystalline and haunting.
叶绍翁 | Ye Shaoweng | 宋 | fl. 13th century | Southern Song poet of the "Jianghu" (rivers and lakes) school.

卢梅坡 | Lu Meipo | 宋 | fl. 13th century | Southern Song poet known for two quatrains on plum blossom and snow.
吴文英 | Wu Wenying | 宋 | c. 1200–c. 1260 | Southern Song *ci* poet of dense, jewelled imagery.
无门慧开 | Wumen Huikai | 宋 | 1183–1260 | Chan (Zen) master, compiler of *The Gateless Gate*, the famous collection of koans.
杜耒 | Du Lei | 宋 | d. 1225 | Southern Song poet remembered for one quatrain about a winter-night guest.
