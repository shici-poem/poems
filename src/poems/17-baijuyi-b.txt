== 长恨歌 | The Song of Everlasting Sorrow
by: 白居易
form: 七古
tags: 爱情 怀古 悼亡 女性 相思
scene: night moon palace clouds mist pavilion lady
汉皇重色思倾国，御宇多年求不得。
杨家有女初长成，养在深闺人未识。
天生丽质难自弃，一朝选在君王侧。
回眸一笑百媚生，六宫粉黛无颜色。
春寒赐浴华清池，温泉水滑洗凝脂。
侍儿扶起娇无力，始是新承恩泽时。
云鬓花颜金步摇，芙蓉帐暖度春宵。
春宵苦短日高起，从此君王不早朝。
承欢侍宴无闲暇，春从春游夜专夜。
后宫佳丽三千人，三千宠爱在一身。
金屋妆成娇侍夜，玉楼宴罢醉和春。
姊妹弟兄皆列土，可怜光彩生门户。
遂令天下父母心，不重生男重生女。
骊宫高处入青云，仙乐风飘处处闻。
缓歌慢舞凝丝竹，尽日君王看不足。
渔阳鼙鼓动地来，惊破霓裳羽衣曲。
九重城阙烟尘生，千乘万骑西南行。
翠华摇摇行复止，西出都门百余里。
六军不发无奈何，宛转蛾眉马前死。
花钿委地无人收，翠翘金雀玉搔头。
君王掩面救不得，回看血泪相和流。
黄埃散漫风萧索，云栈萦纡登剑阁。
峨嵋山下少人行，旌旗无光日色薄。
蜀江水碧蜀山青，圣主朝朝暮暮情。
行宫见月伤心色，夜雨闻铃肠断声。
天旋地转回龙驭，到此踌躇不能去。
马嵬坡下泥土中，不见玉颜空死处。
君臣相顾尽沾衣，东望都门信马归。
归来池苑皆依旧，太液芙蓉未央柳。
芙蓉如面柳如眉，对此如何不泪垂。
春风桃李花开日，秋雨梧桐叶落时。
西宫南内多秋草，落叶满阶红不扫。
梨园弟子白发新，椒房阿监青娥老。
夕殿萤飞思悄然，孤灯挑尽未成眠。
迟迟钟鼓初长夜，耿耿星河欲曙天。
鸳鸯瓦冷霜华重，翡翠衾寒谁与共。
悠悠生死别经年，魂魄不曾来入梦。
临邛道士鸿都客，能以精诚致魂魄。
为感君王辗转思，遂教方士殷勤觅。
排空驭气奔如电，升天入地求之遍。
上穷碧落下黄泉，两处茫茫皆不见。
忽闻海上有仙山，山在虚无缥渺间。
楼阁玲珑五云起，其中绰约多仙子。
中有一人字太真，雪肤花貌参差是。
金阙西厢叩玉扃，转教小玉报双成。
闻道汉家天子使，九华帐里梦魂惊。
揽衣推枕起徘徊，珠箔银屏迤逦开。
云鬓半偏新睡觉，花冠不整下堂来。
风吹仙袂飘飘举，犹似霓裳羽衣舞。
玉容寂寞泪阑干，梨花一枝春带雨。
含情凝睇谢君王，一别音容两渺茫。
昭阳殿里恩爱绝，蓬莱宫中日月长。
回头下望人寰处，不见长安见尘雾。
惟将旧物表深情，钿合金钗寄将去。
钗留一股合一扇，钗擘黄金合分钿。
但教心似金钿坚，天上人间会相见。
临别殷勤重寄词，词中有誓两心知。
七月七日长生殿，夜半无人私语时。
在天愿作比翼鸟，在地愿为连理枝。
天长地久有时尽，此恨绵绵无绝期。
--
The Han emperor prized beauty, longed for one to topple states;
Through all his years upon the throne, he sought and found no mate.
The Yang clan had a daughter who was just in her first bloom,
Raised deep within the women's rooms, unknown beyond her room.
But beauty given by heaven is hard to hide away:
She was chosen for the sovereign's side one day.
She turned her head and smiled — a hundred charms were born;
The painted ladies of the Six Palaces looked colourless and worn.
In the chill of spring he let her bathe in Huaqing's waters fine;
The silken springs washed over skin like cream, divine.
Her maids helped her up, so delicate, so weak —
That was when the emperor's favour first touched her cheek.
Cloud-like hair, a flowered face, a golden hairpin's swing;
Behind warm lotus curtains they spent the nights of spring.
Spring nights were all too short; he rose when the sun was high;
From then on he held no morning court — he let the dawns go by.
She shared his feasts and pleasures, never with an hour to rest;
In spring she joined his spring rides; night after night she was his guest.
Three thousand beauties filled the inner palace halls;
The love meant for three thousand on one woman falls.
In her Golden House, made up, she charmed him through the night;
At the Jade Tower, the feasting done, she was drunk with spring's delight.
Her sisters and her brothers all were granted lands and fame —
How dazzling was the glory shining on her family's name!
So parents everywhere beneath the sky now changed their mind:
Not sons, but daughters, were the children they most hoped to find.
High on Mount Li, the palace rose into the blue clouds' height;
Immortal music on the wind was heard there, day and night.
Slow songs, unhurried dances held the strings and flutes in thrall;
All day the sovereign watched, and never had enough at all.
Then the war-drums of Yuyang came, shaking the ground,
And shattered "Rainbow Skirt and Feathered Coat" with their sound.
Smoke and dust rose from the nine-walled city's towers;
A thousand chariots, ten thousand riders fled southwest in those hours.
The kingfisher banners swayed; they marched and stopped once more,
A hundred li out west, beyond the capital's door.
The Six Armies would not march — what could be done?
Before the horses, the moth-browed beauty died, her struggle done.
Her flowered ornaments lay where they fell, and no one gathered them:
Kingfisher plumes, gold sparrows, and a jade pin's broken stem.
The sovereign hid his face — he could not save her, try as he might;
Looking back, his tears and blood flowed mingled at the sight.
Yellow dust blew all around; the wind was bleak and sere;
By cloud-high plank roads winding, they climbed to Sword Pavilion here.
Below Mount Emei, few travellers came by;
The banners lost their lustre, and the sun grew pale in the sky.
The rivers of Shu are green, the mountains of Shu are blue;
Morning and evening, the sovereign's longing grew.
In his travelling palace, the moon was heartbreak to see;
In night rain, the harness bells rang out in agony.
The world turned round; the dragon carriage was coming home at last;
Reaching here, he hesitated, and could not go past.
Beneath the slope of Mawei, in the mud and clay,
No jade face could be seen — just the empty place she lay.
Lord and ministers looked at each other, tears soaking every gown;
Eastward, they let the horses find their own way back to town.
Returning, the pools and gardens were all as they had been —
The lotus of Taiye Lake, the willows of Weiyang green.
The lotus was like her face, the willow like her brow —
Facing them, how could his tears not fall now?
In spring winds, when the peach and plum trees bloom;
In autumn rains, when wutong leaves fall through the gloom;
The Western Palace and the Southern Court were deep in autumn grass;
Red leaves lay thick upon the steps, unswept, as seasons pass.
The players of the Pear Garden had hair turned newly white;
The maids of the Pepper Chamber had lost their youthful light.
In the evening hall fireflies flitted; he sat in silent thought;
He trimmed his lonely lamp till it burned out — but sleep came not.
Slow, slow the bells and drums began the long night's span;
Bright, bright the Milky Way, before the dawn began.
The mandarin-duck roof tiles were cold, the frost lay thick and fair;
The kingfisher quilt was chill — and who was there to share?
Distant, distant, life and death apart through all the years —
Her soul had never come into his dreams to dry his tears.
A Daoist priest from Linqiong, a guest at the capital gate,
Could summon spirits of the dead by his devotion's weight.
Moved by the sovereign's tossing, turning longing and his pain,
They sent this master out to search with all his might and main.
He split the sky and rode the wind, as swift as lightning flies;
He searched the depths beneath the earth, and searched the highest skies —
Up to the blue void, down to the Yellow Springs below:
Both were vast and empty; she was nowhere, high or low.
Then he heard of an immortal mountain in the sea,
A mountain set amid the void, in misty mystery.
Its exquisite towers rose where five-coloured clouds unfurled;
Within lived many graceful fairies, far beyond the world.
And one among them bore the name of Taizhen, so they said,
With snowy skin and flower face — much like the one now dead.
At the golden palace's west wing, he knocked on the door of jade,
And asked Little Jade to tell Shuangcheng, who would tell the lady's maid.
Hearing that an envoy of the Han emperor had come,
Within her nine-flowered curtains, her dreaming soul was struck dumb.
She pushed her pillow, caught her robe, rose up and paced the floor;
The pearl blinds and the silver screens unfolded, door by door.
Her cloud of hair hung half aside — she had only just awoken;
Her flower crown askew, she came down the hall, her calm all broken.
The wind blew her immortal sleeves; they fluttered as she swayed,
As though "Rainbow Skirt and Feathered Coat" were being played.
Her jade-white face was lonely, tears streaming without end —
A spray of pear blossom in spring rain, beneath the drops to bend.
Gazing with deep feeling, she thanked her sovereign lord:
"Since we parted, voice and face are lost — no word, no sight restored.
In Zhaoyang Hall, our love and favour have been cut;
In Penglai Palace, days and months are long, the door is shut.
I turn my head and look down toward the world of men —
I cannot see Chang'an, only dust and mist again.
Let these old keepsakes show you that my love is true:
This inlaid box and golden hairpin — take them back with you.
One prong of the hairpin I keep, one half of the box I hold:
I break the hairpin's gold in two, and split the inlaid gold.
If only our hearts stay as firm as gold and inlay are,
In heaven or on earth we'll meet again, however far."
At parting she sent words again, with care, for him to bear;
In them was a vow known only to the two who'd shared it there:
"On the seventh of the seventh month, in the Hall of Long Life,
At midnight, when no one was near, we whispered, man and wife:
'In heaven, may we be two birds that fly wing to wing;
On earth, two trees whose branches intertwine and cling.'"
Heaven is long and earth endures, yet both will one day end —
But this sorrow will go on forever, and never mend.
--
Written in 806, when Bai Juyi was thirty-four: the love of Emperor Xuanzong (thinly disguised as "the Han emperor") and Consort Yang Guifei — her rise, the An Lushan rebellion (the "war-drums of Yuyang"), the flight to Sichuan, the mutinous troops at Mawei who demanded her death, the emperor's endless grief, and a Daoist magician who finds her soul on an island of immortals. Her vow — to be "birds flying wing to wing, branches intertwined" (比翼鸟, 连理枝) — is the most famous pledge of love in Chinese, and the closing couplet gave the poem its title.
