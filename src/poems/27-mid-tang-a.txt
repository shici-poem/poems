== 江雪 | River Snow
by: 柳宗元
form: 五绝
tags: 雪 冬 隐逸 江河
scene: winter snow mountains river fisher
千山鸟飞绝，万径人踪灭。
孤舟蓑笠翁，独钓寒江雪。
--
A thousand hills — no bird in flight;
Ten thousand paths — no human trace.
One boat; one old man, straw-caped, alone,
Fishing the snow on the cold river's face.
--
Written in exile at Yongzhou (Hunan) after 805. The first characters of the four lines spell 千万孤独, "utterly alone". In a world emptied of life and buried in snow, one old fisherman sits unmoved — Liu Zongyuan's image of his own lonely integrity, and one of the most painted poems in Chinese art.

== 渔翁 | The Old Fisherman
by: 柳宗元
form: 七古
tags: 隐逸 山水 江河 闲适
scene: dawn mist river fisher mountains clouds
渔翁夜傍西岩宿，晓汲清湘燃楚竹。
烟销日出不见人，欸乃一声山水绿。
回看天际下中流，岩上无心云相逐。
--
The fisherman beneath the western cliff spends the night;
At dawn he draws the clear Xiang and burns Chu bamboo.
The mist melts, the sun climbs — not a soul in sight;
"Ai-nai!" — one oar-song, and the hills and streams turn green and new.
Looking back to the sky's edge, he drifts down midstream;
Above the cliffs, the carefree clouds chase each other through.
--
"Ai-nai" (欸乃) is the creak of an oar or a boatman's call; at that sound the landscape seems to spring into colour as the mist clears — one of the most magical lines in Chinese. The aimless, "heartless" clouds (无心) are the fisherman's — and the exiled poet's — freedom from desire.

== 早春呈水部张十八员外（其一） | Early Spring: To Zhang the Eighteenth of the Ministry of Waters
by: 韩愈
form: 七绝
tags: 春 雨 柳 友情
scene: spring rain grass willow wall
天街小雨润如酥，草色遥看近却无。
最是一年春好处，绝胜烟柳满皇都。
--
The fine rain on the royal streets is soft as cream;
The grass, from far away, looks green — up close, there's none.
Of all the year, spring's best is now, I deem —
Better than misty willows veiling the capital later on.
--
Han Yu, aged fifty-six, to his friend Zhang Ji. Line two is a famous observation: new grass so faint that you see it as a green haze from afar, but not when you look at your feet. Early spring, barely begun, is more precious than full spring.

== 左迁至蓝关示侄孙湘 | Demoted, at Lan Pass: To My Grand-Nephew Xiang
by: 韩愈
form: 七律
tags: 失意 爱国 雪 羁旅 冬
scene: winter snow mountains clouds rider horse
一封朝奏九重天，夕贬潮州路八千。
欲为圣明除弊事，肯将衰朽惜残年！
云横秦岭家何在？雪拥蓝关马不前。
知汝远来应有意，好收吾骨瘴江边。
--
At dawn I sent one memorial to the Ninefold Gate;
By dusk I was banished to Chaozhou, eight thousand li.
I sought to rid our wise lord of a harmful state —
Would I, old and worn, begrudge what years are left to me?
Clouds lie across the Qinling — where now is my home?
Snow chokes the Lan Pass, and my horse won't take a step.
I know you've followed me so far with purpose:
To gather up my bones beside the fever-river's edge.
--
In 819 Emperor Xianzong had a finger-bone of the Buddha paraded into the palace. Han Yu, a stern Confucian, sent a memorial condemning it, was nearly executed, and was banished to Chaozhou on the far southern coast. At the snowbound Lan Pass his grand-nephew Han Xiang caught up with him. Expecting to die in the malarial south, he asks the young man to collect his bones. (He survived, and was recalled within a year.)

== 晚春 | Late Spring
by: 韩愈
form: 七绝
tags: 春 花 哲理 风
scene: spring flowers petals willow wind
草树知春不久归，百般红紫斗芳菲。
杨花榆荚无才思，惟解漫天作雪飞。
--
The grass and trees know spring will not stay long,
And vie in every red and purple, blooming bright.
The willow-down and elm-seeds lack the talent of that throng —
They only know to fill the sky like snowflakes in their flight.
--
Flowers compete in beauty before spring leaves, while plain willow catkins and elm seeds, with no colour to show, simply fill the sky like snow. Mockery — or quiet admiration for those who give what they can? Readers have argued both ways.

== 游子吟 | The Wanderer's Song
by: 孟郊
form: 乐府
tags: 人生 思乡 离别 春
scene: night candle cottage lady window
慈母手中线，游子身上衣。
临行密密缝，意恐迟迟归。
谁言寸草心，报得三春晖。
--
The thread in a loving mother's hand
Becomes the coat her wandering son will wear.
Before he goes, she stitches close and tight,
Afraid he'll long be gone, away from her care.
Who says a blade of grass, an inch in height,
Could ever repay the spring sun's light so fair?
--
The most beloved poem in Chinese about a mother's love. Meng Jiao wrote it at fifty, when he finally received a minor post and could bring his mother to live with him. The child is "an inch of grass"; the mother's love is the sunshine of spring, which no grass can ever repay.

== 登科后 | After Passing the Examination
by: 孟郊
form: 七绝
tags: 春 人生 壮志 花
scene: spring rider horse flowers wall
昔日龌龊不足夸，今朝放荡思无涯。
春风得意马蹄疾，一日看尽长安花。
--
My meagre days of old aren't worth a word;
Today, set free, my thoughts roam without bound.
Proud in the spring wind, my horse's hooves have whirred —
In one day I've seen every flower in Chang'an town.
--
Meng Jiao passed the *jinshi* examination in 796, aged forty-six, after two failures. New graduates paraded through the capital and toured its gardens. Two idioms come from this poem: 春风得意, "proud in the spring wind" (flushed with success), and 走马观花, "viewing flowers from horseback" (seeing things in a hurry).

== 寻隐者不遇 | Seeking the Hermit, Not Finding Him
by: 贾岛
form: 五绝
tags: 隐逸 山 禅
scene: day pine mountains clouds person child
松下问童子，言师采药去。
只在此山中，云深不知处。
--
I asked the boy beneath the pine;
"My master's gone for herbs," he said.
"He's on this mountain, somewhere there —
The clouds are deep; I can't say where."
--
Visiting a hermit and not finding him is a whole genre in Chinese poetry. Three questions are implied, three answers given; the hermit himself, lost somewhere in the clouds, becomes part of the mountain.

== 题李凝幽居 | Written at Li Ning's Secluded Dwelling
by: 贾岛
form: 五律
tags: 隐逸 月 夜 禅
scene: night moon cottage bridge birds lake person
闲居少邻并，草径入荒园。
鸟宿池边树，僧敲月下门。
过桥分野色，移石动云根。
暂去还来此，幽期不负言。
--
His quiet home has hardly any neighbour;
A grassy path leads to the wild garden's gloom.
Birds are roosting in the trees beside the pond;
A monk knocks at the gate beneath the moon.
Across the bridge, the wild hues change and part;
Moving the stones stirs up the roots of cloud.
I leave for now, but will come back again —
I won't break faith with this quiet place, as vowed.
--
The source of the story of 推敲 (*tuīqiāo*, "push–knock"): riding his donkey, Jia Dao could not decide whether the monk should "push" (推) or "knock at" (敲) the gate, and, lost in thought, rode into the procession of the governor Han Yu — who, instead of punishing him, said "knock" was better. Ever since, 推敲 means to weigh one's words with care.
