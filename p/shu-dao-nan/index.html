蜀道难

〔唐〕李白

噫吁嚱，危乎高哉！

蜀道之难，难于上青天！

蚕丛及鱼凫，开国何茫然！

尔来四万八千岁，不与秦塞通人烟。

西当太白有鸟道，可以横绝峨眉巅。

地崩山摧壮士死，然后天梯石栈相钩连。

上有六龙回日之高标，下有冲波逆折之回川。

黄鹤之飞尚不得过，猿猱欲度愁攀援。

青泥何盘盘，百步九折萦岩峦。

扪参历井仰胁息，以手抚膺坐长叹。

问君西游何时还？畏途巉岩不可攀。

但见悲鸟号古木，雄飞雌从绕林间。

又闻子规啼夜月，愁空山。

蜀道之难，难于上青天，使人听此凋朱颜！

连峰去天不盈尺，枯松倒挂倚绝壁。

飞湍瀑流争喧豗，砯崖转石万壑雷。

其险也如此，嗟尔远道之人胡为乎来哉！

剑阁峥嵘而崔嵬，一夫当关，万夫莫开。

所守或匪亲，化为狼与豺。

朝避猛虎，夕避长蛇；磨牙吮血，杀人如麻。

锦城虽云乐，不如早还家。

蜀道之难，难于上青天，侧身西望长咨嗟！

英译 English translation

The Road to Shu Is Hard

Shǔ Dào Nán
Li Bai · Tang · Yuefu ballad

Ah! Oh! How steep! How high!

The road to Shu is hard — harder than climbing the blue sky!

Cancong and Yufu, who founded the land —

How dim and distant, those days, to understand!

For forty-eight thousand years since then,

No smoke of hearths crossed to the Qin frontier's men.

West, by Mount Taibai, runs a track only birds can trace,

That cuts across to Emei's summit face.

Then the earth split, the mountain fell, the strong men died,

And ladders to heaven, stone plank-roads, were linked along the side.

Above, a peak so high the sun's six dragons turn their car;

Below, the rapids dash and twist where the whirling eddies are.

The yellow crane in flight can never cross that height;

The gibbons long to pass, and dread the climb outright.

How Green Mud Ridge goes coiling, coiling round —

Nine turns in a hundred steps, through crags it's wound!

You touch Orion, pass the Well Star, gasp and look on high;

You sit and clutch your chest, and heave a long, long sigh.

I ask you, traveller to the west, when will you come back home?

The road is fearful, sheer the cliffs — no foot can overcome.

You see only mournful birds that cry in ancient trees,

The male in front, the female after, circling through the leaves;

You hear the cuckoo weep beneath the moon at night,

Grieving the empty hills.

The road to Shu is hard — harder than climbing the blue sky!

Just hearing of it, rosy cheeks would fade and youth would fly.

Peak after peak, not a foot from heaven's floor,

Dead pines hang upside down from cliffs they cling to more and more.

The flying torrents, roaring falls, compete in their uproar;

They pound the cliffs and roll the rocks — ten thousand gorges' thunder-roar.

Such is its peril!

Alas, you traveller from afar, why ever come this way?

Sword Gate Pass looms, jagged and steep:

One man can hold it — ten thousand cannot break in to sweep.

And if its guards are not men you can trust,

They'll turn to wolves and jackals, as such men must.

By day beware the tiger, by night beware the snake —

They grind their teeth and suck men's blood, and kill like reapers' rake.

Though Brocade City may be fair, they say,

Better go home without delay.

The road to Shu is hard — harder than climbing the blue sky!

I turn and gaze toward the west, and heave a long, long sigh.

About this poem

Shu is today's Sichuan, cut off from the central plain by mountain ranges crossed only on plank roads pinned to cliff faces. Li Bai, a son of Shu, mixes legend (the ancient kings Cancong and Yufu; five strongmen killed when a mountain collapsed as they dragged a snake from its cave; the six dragons pulling the sun's chariot) with real geography, in wildly irregular lines that lurch like the road itself. When He Zhizhang read it, he called Li Bai "an immortal banished from heaven".
