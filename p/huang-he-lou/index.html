黄鹤楼

〔唐〕崔颢

昔人已乘黄鹤去，此地空余黄鹤楼。

黄鹤一去不复返，白云千载空悠悠。

晴川历历汉阳树，芳草萋萋鹦鹉洲。

日暮乡关何处是？烟波江上使人愁。

英译 English translation

Yellow Crane Tower

Huáng Hè Lóu
Cui Hao · Tang · Seven-character regulated verse

The sage of old has ridden off upon the yellow crane;

Here only Yellow Crane Tower is left, standing alone.

The yellow crane, once gone, has never come again;

For a thousand years white clouds drift on, aimless, on their own.

Across the sunlit river, the trees of Hanyang are clear;

On Parrot Isle the fragrant grasses grow so thick and green.

The sun goes down — where is my home? It isn't here;

The mist upon the river's waves brings sorrow to the scene.

About this poem

Legend says an immortal flew away from this spot in Wuhan on a yellow crane. Cui Hao's poem breaks the rules of regulated verse — "yellow crane" appears three times in four lines — yet it was hailed as the greatest seven-character regulated poem of the Tang. Li Bai, visiting the tower, is said to have sighed: "Before my eyes is a scene I cannot describe, for Cui Hao's poem is already above it."

Earlier translations 旧译

“Home Longings”Herbert A. Giles, 1898

Here a mortal once sailed up to heaven on a crane,

And the Yellow-Crane Kiosque will for ever remain;

But the bird flew away and will come back no more,

Though the white clouds are there as the white clouds of yore.

Away o'er the river lie Han-yang's fair trees,

From the Parrot Isle's grasses comes a scent-laden breeze,

Yet at dusk my eyes turn to their far-away home,

Beyond the broad River, its waves, and its foam.

Translated by Herbert A. Giles, from Chinese Poetry in English Verse (1898) · public domain. Lightly adapted: lines 5-6 reworded to restore the Hanyang trees and the grass of Parrot Isle (the printed "east" and "west" are not in the Chinese); "daily" changed to "at dusk" in line 7.

Here a mortal once sailed up to heaven on a crane,

And the Yellow-Crane Kiosque will for ever remain;

But the bird flew away and will come back no more,

Though the white clouds are there as the white clouds of yore.

Away to the east lie fair forests of trees,

From the flowers on the west comes a scent-laden breeze,

Yet my eyes daily turn to their far-away home,

Beyond the broad River, its waves, and its foam.

“The Huang Ho Lou”W. J. B. Fletcher, 1919

The sage of old has flown away upon a Yellow Crane,

And left its Tower alone to mark where mortals saw him last.

The Yellow Crane once flown away—it never comes again.

Long years have past—yet white and ghast the empty clouds remain.

Mid winding groves of Hanyang's trees the stream pellucid flows.

On Parrot Isle the fragrant grass in wild luxuriance grows.

My village from my gazes the dying sunbeams part.

The river hid the mist amid calls shadows o'er my heart.

Translated by W. J. B. Fletcher, from Gems of Chinese Verse (1919) · public domain.
