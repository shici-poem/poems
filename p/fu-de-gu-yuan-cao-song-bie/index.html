赋得古原草送别

〔唐〕白居易

离离原上草，一岁一枯荣。

野火烧不尽，春风吹又生。

远芳侵古道，晴翠接荒城。

又送王孙去，萋萋满别情。

英译 English translation

Grass on the Ancient Plain: A Farewell

Fù Dé Gǔ Yuán Cǎo Sòng Bié
Bai Juyi · Tang · Five-character regulated verse

How lush the grass upon the plain!

Each year it withers, then grows green again.

The wildfires cannot burn it all away;

The spring wind blows, and it springs back to stay.

Its distant fragrance creeps across the ancient road;

Its sunlit green runs to the ruined town's abode.

Once more I see a friend go on his way —

The grass grows thick with all I cannot say.

About this poem

Written at sixteen as an exercise on a set theme ("Composed on the Theme…", 赋得). When the young Bai Juyi brought his poems to the capital, the writer Gu Kuang joked about his name — "Juyi" means "living easily", and "rice is dear in Chang'an; living here is not easy" — until he read the second couplet and exclaimed, "With lines like these, living here will be easy!" "Wildfire cannot burn it out; the spring wind blows and it grows again" became a proverb for indomitable life.

Earlier translations 旧译

“The Grass”L. Cranmer-Byng, 1909

How beautiful and fresh the grass returns!

When golden days decline, the meadow burns;

Yet autumn suns no hidden root have slain,

The spring winds blow, and there is grass again.

Green rioting on olden ways it falls:

Sun-bright, it storms the ruined city walls;

Yet since Wang Sun departed long ago,

Each parting finds the lush grass full of woe.

Translated by L. Cranmer-Byng, from A Lute of Jade (1909) · public domain. Lightly adapted: line 6 "The blue sky" changed to "Sun-bright, it" (the Chinese has sunlit green reaching the ruined city); last line reworded to restore the farewell.

How beautiful and fresh the grass returns!

“The Grass”W. J. B. Fletcher, 1919

How densely thick the grass upon the plain!

Decay and splendour one year to it brings.

The wild-fires burn it down—but all in vain—

With each new breath of Spring it lives again.

Its fragrance creeps across the Ancient Ways,

Its sun-lit verdure o'er the ruin strays.

Its growth speeds Nature's lover on his ways.

With wild farewells its long luxuriance rings.

Translated by W. J. B. Fletcher, from Gems of Chinese Verse (1919) · public domain. Lightly adapted: line 3: "corpse-fires" changed to "wild-fires"; the Chinese has prairie fires.
