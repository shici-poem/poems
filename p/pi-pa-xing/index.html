琵琶行

〔唐〕白居易

浔阳江头夜送客，枫叶荻花秋瑟瑟。

主人下马客在船，举酒欲饮无管弦。

醉不成欢惨将别，别时茫茫江浸月。

忽闻水上琵琶声，主人忘归客不发。

寻声暗问弹者谁？

琵琶声停欲语迟。

移船相近邀相见，添酒回灯重开宴。

千呼万唤始出来，犹抱琵琶半遮面。

转轴拨弦三两声，未成曲调先有情。

弦弦掩抑声声思，似诉平生不得志。

低眉信手续续弹，说尽心中无限事。

轻拢慢捻抹复挑，初为《霓裳》后《六幺》。

大弦嘈嘈如急雨，小弦切切如私语。

嘈嘈切切错杂弹，大珠小珠落玉盘。

间关莺语花底滑，幽咽泉流冰下难。

冰泉冷涩弦凝绝，凝绝不通声暂歇。

别有幽愁暗恨生，此时无声胜有声。

银瓶乍破水浆迸，铁骑突出刀枪鸣。

曲终收拨当心画，四弦一声如裂帛。

东船西舫悄无言，唯见江心秋月白。

沉吟放拨插弦中，整顿衣裳起敛容。

自言本是京城女，家在虾蟆陵下住。

十三学得琵琶成，名属教坊第一部。

曲罢曾教善才服，妆成每被秋娘妒。

五陵年少争缠头，一曲红绡不知数。

钿头银篦击节碎，血色罗裙翻酒污。

今年欢笑复明年，秋月春风等闲度。

弟走从军阿姨死，暮去朝来颜色故。

门前冷落鞍马稀，老大嫁作商人妇。

商人重利轻别离，前月浮梁买茶去。

去来江口守空船，绕船月明江水寒。

夜深忽梦少年事，梦啼妆泪红阑干。

我闻琵琶已叹息，又闻此语重唧唧。

同是天涯沦落人，相逢何必曾相识！

我从去年辞帝京，谪居卧病浔阳城。

浔阳地僻无音乐，终岁不闻丝竹声。

住近湓江地低湿，黄芦苦竹绕宅生。

其间旦暮闻何物？

杜鹃啼血猿哀鸣。

春江花朝秋月夜，往往取酒还独倾。

岂无山歌与村笛？

呕哑嘲哳难为听。

今夜闻君琵琶语，如听仙乐耳暂明。

莫辞更坐弹一曲，为君翻作《琵琶行》。

感我此言良久立，却坐促弦弦转急。

凄凄不似向前声，满座重闻皆掩泣。

座中泣下谁最多？

江州司马青衫湿。

英译 English translation

Song of the Pipa Player

Pí Pá Xíng
Bai Juyi · Tang · Seven-character old-style verse

By the Xunyang River, at night, I saw a guest away;

Maple leaves and reed flowers rustled in the autumn grey.

I, the host, dismounted; my guest was in his boat;

We raised our cups to drink — but there was no music, not one note.

Drunk, yet joyless, sadly we were about to part;

At parting, the moon lay steeped in the boundless river's heart.

Then suddenly, across the water, came a pipa's sound;

The host forgot to leave; the guest stayed, outward-bound.

We followed the sound and softly asked who it was that played;

The pipa stopped; she was slow to answer, and delayed.

We moved our boat beside hers and asked her to appear;

We poured more wine, relit the lamps, and spread the feast with cheer.

At last, after a thousand pleas, she came out with slow grace,

Still holding up her pipa so it half-concealed her face.

She turned the pegs and plucked the strings — a note or two, or three;

Before a tune had even formed, there was feeling, plain to see.

Each string was muffled, every note was heavy with thought,

As if she told a lifetime's grief, of hopes that came to naught.

Head bowed, she let her hand play on, continuing on and on,

And told the countless sorrows of her heart, one by one.

Lightly she pressed and slowly twisted, swept, and plucked the strings:

First "Rainbow Skirt", then "Green Waist" — the tunes of courts and kings.

The heavy strings roared like a sudden squall of rain;

The fine strings murmured like lovers' whispers, low and plain.

Roaring and murmuring, interwoven as she played —

Like pearls, both large and small, that fall upon a plate of jade.

Now orioles chattered, liquid, gliding under flowers;

Now a choked spring sobbed, struggling under ice for hours.

The icy spring grew cold and halting; the strings congealed and died;

Congealed, cut off, the sound fell still — the music paused, held still inside.

A different, deeper sorrow, a hidden grief, was born:

Now silence spoke more eloquently than sound — and more forlorn.

Then suddenly a silver vase burst, and water splashed about;

Armoured horsemen charged, and swords and spears rang out.

The tune ended: she swept her plectrum across the strings' heart;

The four strings cried as one, like silk being torn apart.

On boats to east and west, a hush; no one spoke at all —

Only the autumn moon, white in mid-river, over all.

Thoughtfully she laid the plectrum down between the strings,

Straightened her clothes, rose, composed her face, and spoke of things:

"I was a girl of the capital once," she said. "My home

Was down by Toad Mound — that's where I used to roam.

At thirteen I had learned to play the pipa with full skill;

My name stood in the first rank of the Music Academy's bill.

My playing made the master musicians bow in admiration;

My beauty, when made up, roused even Qiuniang's indignation.

The young lords of the Five Tombs vied to shower me with gifts;

For one song, rolls of red silk past counting, piled in drifts.

Silver combs with jewelled heads broke as they beat the time;

Blood-red silk skirts were stained with spilt wine — I was in my prime.

This year was laughter, and the next year laughter too;

Autumn moons and spring breezes passed, and idly through.

My brother joined the army, and the madam passed away;

Evenings went and mornings came; my looks faded day by day.

Before my door it grew deserted; fewer horses came;

Grown old, I married a merchant, and took a merchant's name.

A merchant cares for profit, and thinks little of goodbye;

Last month he went to Fuliang to buy tea, and left me by.

Since then, at the river's mouth, I guard the empty boat;

Around it, bright moonlight; the river cold, remote.

Late at night I suddenly dreamed of my young days;

I wept in my dream, and red tears through my rouge ran in a glaze."

Hearing the pipa, I had sighed already, deep;

Hearing her story, I sighed again, and began to weep.

We both are outcasts, drifting at the far ends of the earth;

Meeting like this, what need to have known each other since our birth?

"Since last year I left the capital, I've lived in banishment;

I lie here sick in Xunyang town, where I was demoted and sent.

Xunyang is remote, and there's no music to be found;

All year I've never heard the strings or flutes resound.

I live beside the Pen, where the ground is low and damp;

Yellow reeds and bitter bamboo surround my house like a camp.

What do I hear here, morning and evening long?

The cuckoo crying blood, the gibbons' mournful song.

On spring river mornings full of flowers, on autumn nights of moon,

I often take my wine and drink alone, and end too soon.

Of course there are hill songs and village flutes — but they

Are shrill and harsh and croaking, painful when they play.

Tonight I heard your pipa speak, and it seemed

Like heavenly music — my ears for a while were redeemed.

Don't refuse; sit down again and play us one more song,

And I will write for you 'The Ballad of the Pipa' before long."

Moved by what I said, she stood a long while there,

Then sat, and tightened the strings; the notes grew quick and spare.

So mournful, so unlike the music played before,

That all who heard it hid their faces, weeping once more.

And of them all, whose tears were falling most? I'd say

The Marshal of Jiangzhou's — his blue robe soaked that day.

About this poem

Written in 816 at Jiangzhou (Jiujiang), where Bai Juyi had been demoted to the empty post of marshal (司马). Seeing a friend off on the river at night, he hears a former star musician of the capital, now a merchant's neglected wife. The description of her playing — "large pearls and small pearls falling on a plate of jade", "now silence was more eloquent than sound", "a silver vase bursting" — is the most famous evocation of music in Chinese. "We are both outcasts at the ends of the earth; why must we have known each other before?" became the motto of every exile. Blue-green robes were worn by the lowest-ranking officials.

Earlier translations 旧译

“The Lute Girl”L. Cranmer-Byng, 1909

By night, beside the river, underneath

The flower-like maple leaves that bloom alone

In autumn's silent revels of decay,

We said farewell. The host, dismounting, sped

The parting guest whose boat rocked under him,

And when the circling stirrup-cup went round,

No light guitar, no lute, was heard again;

But on the heart aglow with wine there fell

Beneath the cold bright moon the cold adieu

Of fading friends—when suddenly beyond

The cradled waters stole the lullaby

Of some faint lute; then host forgot to go,

Guest lingered on: all, wondering at the spell,

Besought the dim enchantress to reveal

Her presence; but the music died and gave

No answer, dying. Then a boat shot forth

To bring the shy musician to the shore.

Cups were refilled and lanterns trimmed again,

And so the festival went on. At last,

Slow yielding to their prayers, the stranger came,

Hiding her burning face behind her lute;

And twice her hand essayed the strings, and twice

She faltered in her task; then tenderly,

As for an old sad tale of hopeless years,

With drooping head and fingers deft she poured

Her soul forth into melodies. Now slow

The plectrum led to prayer the cloistered chords,

Now loudly with the crash of falling rain,

Now soft as the leaf whispering of words,

Now loud and soft together as the long

Patter of pearls and seed-pearls on a dish

Of marble; liquid now as from the bush

Warbles the mango bird; meandering

Now as the streamlet seawards; voiceless now

As the wild torrent in the strangling arms

Of her ice-lover, lying motionless,

Lulled in a passion far too deep for sound.

Then as the water from the broken vase

Gushes, or on the mailed horseman falls

The anvil din of steel, as on the silk

The slash of rending, so upon the strings

Her plectrum fell. . . .

Then silence over us.

No sound broke the charmed air. The autumn moon

Swam silver o'er the tide, as with a sigh

The stranger stirred to go.

"I passed," said she,

"My childhood in the capital; my home

Was near the hills. A girl of twelve, I learnt

The magic of the lute, the passionate

Blending of lute and voice that drew the souls

Of the great masters to acknowledgment;

And lovely women, envious of my face,

Bowed at the shrine in secret. The young lords

Vied for a look's approval. One brief song

Brought many costly bales. Gold ornaments

And silver pins were smashed and trodden down,

And blood-red silken skirts were stained with wine

In oft-times echoing applause. And so

I laughed my life away from year to year

While the spring breezes and the autumn moon

Caressed my careless head. Then on a day

My brother sought the battles in Kansuh;

My mother died: nights passed and mornings came,

And with them waned my beauty. Now no more

My doors were thronged; few were the cavaliers

That lingered by my side; so I became

A trader's wife, the chattel of a slave

Whose lord was gold, who, parting, little recked

Of separation and the unhonoured bride.

Since the tenth moon was full my husband went

To where the tea-fields ripen. I remained,

To wander in my little lonely boat

Over the cold bright wave o' nights, and dream

Of the dead days, the haze of happy days,

And see them set again in dreams and tears."

Already the sweet sorrows of her lute

Had moved my soul to pity; now these words

Pierced me the heart. "O lady fair," I cried,

"We are the vagrants of the world, and need

No ceremony to be friends. Last year

I left the Imperial City, banished far

To this plague-stricken spot, where desolation

Broods on from year to heavy year, nor lute

Nor love's guitar is heard. By marshy bank

Girt with tall yellow reeds and dwarf bamboos

I dwell. Night long and day no stir, no sound,

Only the lurking cuckoo's blood-stained note,

The gibbon's mournful wail. Hill songs I have,

And village pipes with their discordant twang.

But now I listen to thy lute methinks

The gods were parents to thy music. Sit

And sing to us again, while I engrave

Thy story on my tablets!" Gratefully

(For long she had been standing) the lute girl

Sat down and passed into another song,

Sad and so soft, a dream, unlike the song

Of now ago. Then all her hearers wept

In sorrow unrestrained; and I the more,

Weeping until the pale chrysanthemums

Upon my darkened robe were starred with dew.

Translated by L. Cranmer-Byng, from A Lute of Jade (1909) · public domain.
