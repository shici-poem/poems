月夜忆舍弟

〔唐〕杜甫

戍鼓断人行，边秋一雁声。

露从今夜白，月是故乡明。

有弟皆分散，无家问死生。

寄书长不达，况乃未休兵。

英译 English translation

Thinking of My Brothers on a Moonlit Night

Yuè Yè Yì Shè Dì
Du Fu · Tang · Five-character regulated verse

The garrison drums have stopped all travellers on their way;

At the autumn frontier, one wild goose cries in the night.

From tonight on, the dew turns white, as the old seasons say;

The moon, I know, at home is shining far more bright.

I have brothers, but they're scattered far apart;

No home is left to ask if they're alive or dead.

The letters that I send never reach their mark —

And still the wars go on, and still no peace ahead.

About this poem

Qinzhou, autumn 759. The night is "White Dew" (白露), one of the twenty-four solar terms, marking the turn into autumn. A single wild goose — geese fly in families, and stand for brothers — cries alone. "The moon is brighter over my hometown" became a proverb: everything is better at home.

Earlier translations 旧译

“To His Brother”Herbert A. Giles, 1898

The evening drum has emptied every street,

One autumn goose screams on its frontier flight,

The crystal dew is glittering at my feet.

The moon sheds, as of old, her silvery light.

The brothers,—ah, where are they? Scattered each;

No home whence one might learn the other's harms.

Letters have oft miscarried: shall they reach

Now when the land rings with the clash of arms?

Translated by Herbert A. Giles, from Chinese Poetry in English Verse (1898) · public domain.

“Thinking of My Brothers on a Moonlit Night”W. J. B. Fletcher, 1919

The war drum booms: all roads are bare.

One wild goose clangs: 'tis Autumn there.

Our nightly dews hence will be white.

On our old home the Moon is bright.

Brothers have I all scattered far.

Homeless, how know if still they are?

Letters I send: but none reply.

Is this not War's sad tyranny?

Translated by W. J. B. Fletcher, from Gems of Chinese Verse (1919) · public domain.
