关雎

〔先秦〕诗经

关关雎鸠，在河之洲。

窈窕淑女，君子好逑。

参差荇菜，左右流之。

窈窕淑女，寤寐求之。

求之不得，寤寐思服。

悠哉悠哉，辗转反侧。

参差荇菜，左右采之。

窈窕淑女，琴瑟友之。

参差荇菜，左右芼之。

窈窕淑女，钟鼓乐之。

英译 English translation

The Ospreys Cry

Guān Jū
The Book of Songs · Pre-Qin · Four-character verse

"Guan-guan!" the ospreys cry

On the islet in the stream.

A lovely, modest maiden —

A fine lord's bride, his dream.

The water-plants grow long and short;

She gathers left and right.

The lovely, modest maiden —

He seeks her day and night.

He seeks her and can't win her;

Waking, sleeping, she's in his mind.

Longing, longing, on and on,

He tosses, turns — no rest to find.

The water-plants grow long and short;

She picks them left and right.

The lovely, modest maiden —

With zither and lute he'll court her, as is right.

The water-plants grow long and short;

She sorts them left and right.

The lovely, modest maiden —

With bells and drums he'll bring her delight.

About this poem

The first poem of the Book of Songs, and so of all Chinese poetry — about three thousand years old. Ospreys, believed to mate for life, call to each other; a young man falls for a girl gathering water plants and dreams of winning her with music. Confucius said it showed "joy without excess, sorrow without harm". 窈窕淑女，君子好逑 is still the classic phrase for a perfect match.

Earlier translations 旧译

“Kwan-ts‘eu”James Legge, 1876

Hark! from the islet in the stream the voice

Of the fish-hawks that o’er their nest rejoice!

From them our thoughts to that young lady go,

Modest and virtuous, loth herself to show.

Where could be found, to share our prince’s state,

So fair, so virtuous, and so fit a mate?

See how the duckweed’s stalks, or short or long,

Sway left and right, as moves the current strong!

So hard it was for him the maid to find!

By day, by night, our prince with constant mind

Sought for her long, but all his search was vain.

Awake, asleep, he ever felt the pain

Of longing thought, as when on restless bed,

Tossing about, one turns his fevered head.

Here long, there short, afloat the duckweed lies;

But caught at last, we seize the longed-for prize.

The maiden modest, virtuous, coy, is found;—

Strike every lute, and joyous welcome sound.

Ours now, the duckweed from the stream we bear,

And cook to use with other viands rare.

He has the maiden, modest, virtuous, bright;

Let bells and drums proclaim our great delight.

Translated by James Legge, from The She King; or, The Book of Ancient Poetry, Translated in English Verse (1876) · public domain.
