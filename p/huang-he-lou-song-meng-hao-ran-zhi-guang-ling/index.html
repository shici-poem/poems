黄鹤楼送孟浩然之广陵

〔唐〕李白

故人西辞黄鹤楼，烟花三月下扬州。

孤帆远影碧空尽，唯见长江天际流。

英译 English translation

Seeing Meng Haoran Off to Guangling at Yellow Crane Tower

Huáng Hè Lóu Sòng Mèng Hào Rán Zhī Guǎng Líng
Li Bai · Tang · Seven-character quatrain

My old friend bids the Yellow Crane Tower in the west goodbye;

In the third month's mist of blossom, down to Yangzhou he goes.

His lonely sail, a fading shade, is lost in the blue sky;

Now only the Long River to the edge of heaven flows.

About this poem

Meng Haoran, twelve years older, was a poet Li Bai idolised. The poem names no feeling at all — Li Bai simply stands watching until the sail vanishes, and that long gaze is the friendship. Yangzhou (Guangling) in spring was the most glamorous city of the empire; 烟花 "mist and flowers" became a set phrase for its springtime.

Earlier translations 旧译

“Gone”Herbert A. Giles, 1898

At the Yellow-Crane pagoda, where we stopped to bid adieu,

The mists and flowers of April seemed to wish good speed to you.

Into the blue, your lessening sail had vanished from my eye,

And left me with the River, rolling onward to the sky.

Translated by Herbert A. Giles, from Chinese Poetry in English Verse (1898) · public domain. Lightly adapted: "At the Emerald Isle" changed to "Into the blue" in line 3 (the sail vanishes into the blue sky, not at an island).

At the Yellow-Crane pagoda, where we stopped to bid adieu,

The mists and flowers of April seemed to wish good speed to you.

At the Emerald Isle, your lessening sail had vanished from my eye,

And left me with the River, rolling onward to the sky.

“The Huang Ho Lou (A Farewell Ode to Meng Hao-jan)”W. J. B. Fletcher, 1919

You parted; leaving to the West the Huang Ho Lou,

The mists of Spring in floating veils descending on Yangchou.

Adown the distance faded hence with thee yon lonely sail

To where the mournful River's waves into the skyline flow.

Translated by W. J. B. Fletcher, from Gems of Chinese Verse (1919) · public domain.
